Iron rusts when it reacts with oxygen and water. Rust is a reddish-brown layer that flakes away from the metal, and each flake exposes fresh iron underneath. Because of this, a rusty iron gate slowly becomes thinner and weaker. Rusting happens faster near the sea, since salt in the air helps the reaction along.

People protect iron in several ways. Painting a gate stops air and moisture from reaching the iron. Oil and grease do the same job for tools and machine parts. Another method is galvanising, which means coating iron with a thin layer of zinc. Zinc wears away before the iron does, so the iron stays safe even when the coating is scratched.

In 1999, a town in our district repainted its old iron bridge to protect it. Workers first scraped off the loose rust with wire brushes. Then they covered the bridge with a red primer and two coats of grey paint. The town now checks the bridge every year, and it repaints any spot where the paint has cracked. Because of this care, the bridge is still strong today, and children walk across it to reach school every morning.

Stainless steel is different from ordinary iron. It contains chromium, which forms a thin invisible layer on the surface, so spoons and kitchen sinks made of it do not rust easily.
