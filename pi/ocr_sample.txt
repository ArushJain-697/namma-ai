In 1930, salt became the centre of India's struggle for freedom. The British government had made a law that gave it complete control over the making and sale of salt, and it put a tax on salt. Salt is something that every person, rich or poor, needs in their food, so Mahatma Gandhi saw the salt tax as a clear example of unjust British rule. On 12 March 1930, Gandhi set out on foot from Sabarmati Ashram near Ahmedabad with 78 of his followers. They walked for 24 days, covering about 385 kilometres, and stopped at villages on the way, where Gandhi spoke to large crowds. On 6 April they reached Dandi, a small village on the coast of Gujarat. There Gandhi picked up a handful of salt from the seashore, breaking the salt law. This simple act was the beginning of the Civil Disobedience Movement. Across the country, people made salt from sea water, refused to buy foreign cloth and refused to pay taxes. Women took part in large numbers, picketing shops that sold liquor and foreign goods. Thousands of people, including Gandhi, were arrested, but the protests continued peacefully. The Salt March showed that ordinary people, by refusing to obey an unjust law without using violence, could challenge a powerful empire.
